Q1. Stress is the internal resisting force per unit area of a cross-section, σ = F/A, measured in pascals. Strain is the change in length per unit original length, ε = ΔL/L, and it is dimensionless. Stress is the cause (the loading) and strain is the resulting deformation. Hooke's law: up to the limit of proportionality, stress is directly proportional to strain, σ = Eε, where E is Young's modulus, a constant of the material.

Q4. In a tensile test a standard specimen of mild steel is gripped in a testing machine and loaded gradually while the load and the extension are recorded. Up to the limit of proportionality the stress-strain graph is a straight line that obeys Hooke's law, and the deformation is elastic: the specimen goes back to its original length when unloaded. At the yield point the steel starts to deform plastically and the strain increases with little increase in stress, so the deformation is permanent. After strain hardening the curve reaches the ultimate tensile strength, which is the maximum stress. Then necking begins, the cross-section reduces locally and the specimen finally fractures. The strain at fracture shows how ductile the material is.

Q5. Consider a thin-walled cylinder of internal diameter d, length L and thickness t under internal pressure p. Cut it along a diameter and look at one half in equilibrium. The pressure acts on the projected area d × L, so the bursting force is p × d × L. This is resisted by the hoop stress σ acting on the two cut walls, each of area t × L, so the resisting force is 2σtL. For equilibrium 2σtL = pdL, hence σ = pd/(2t).

Q6. Ductile materials such as mild steel deform visibly before they fail, so an overload gives a warning and the structure can be repaired or cleared before it collapses. They also absorb a lot of energy through plastic deformation and can redistribute load around stress concentrations. A brittle material such as cast iron fractures suddenly with little warning.
