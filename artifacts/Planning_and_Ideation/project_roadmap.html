1. Problem and scope

The problem. A teacher has a question paper, model answers, sometimes a marking scheme, and a pile of student answer sheets. Marking takes hours, and the student usually gets back a number and a few ticks. We want a system that reads each answer sheet and returns a mark for every question plus feedback the student can learn from.

What it must do

  • Give a fair mark per question, judged against the model answer and marking scheme.
  • Explain each mark: what was right, what lost marks and why, what to do next time.
  • Run start to finish with no human involved.

In scope now

  • Typed answers.
  • STEM first: explanations, short derivations, numerical working.
  • A few domains, done well, before adding more.

Not now

  • Handwriting (stage 3).
  • Drawn diagrams and graphs.
  • Plagiarism, curving, a teacher review screen.

About "no human"

At run time nobody checks anything. During development we may read a sample of outputs to spot problems. We do not hand-label data. The checks we build (section 6) are automatic so that the system can later run unattended.

2. How it works

Six steps per exam. Steps marked "code" are ordinary programs, which are cheap and never make arithmetic mistakes. Steps marked "AI" call the model.

  1. Read the examcodeSplit the question paper, model answers and each student's sheet into question-by-question pieces.
  2. Get the marking schemeAIIf the exam has one, use it. If not, the AI writes one from the question and model answer: a short list of points, each worth some marks, adding up to the question's total. This happens once per question and is reused for every student. It is then tested automatically (section 6).
  3. Mark each answerAIFor each point in the scheme, the AI says met, partly met or not met, and quotes the student's own words as proof. One call per question covers all points.
  4. Check the markingcodeConfirm every quote really appears in the answer, marks do not exceed the limit, and the total is added up by the program, not the AI. If a check fails, mark that answer once more.
  5. Write the feedbackAIUsing only the checked marking, write for each question: what you did well, which points you missed and why, and what to change. It points at the student's actual words.
  6. Produce the reportcodeOne report per student: marks per question, total, feedback.

Why this shape

Marking against a list of small points is more consistent than asking for one overall mark. A physics study found this clearly, and found the wording of the scheme mattered more than prompt tricks.

Giving the AI the model answer helps. Giving it a wrong one hurts, so model answers must come from the dataset or a trusted source.

Quoting the student forces the AI to point at evidence, and lets code catch invented quotes.

Writing feedback after the marks are checked means the comments cannot contradict the marks.

Cost: about two AI calls per answer (mark, feedback), plus one or two per question for the scheme, paid once.

3. Stages and datasets

We finish a stage before starting the next. Every dataset below was chosen because it already has model answers or a marking scheme, so nobody has to annotate anything. Full details are in DATASETS.md.

STAGE 1

Prove the pipeline

Computer science and networking answers
Datasets
  • DigiKlausur: neural networks exam, 646 answers, model answers, marks 0 to 2. Open licence.
  • SAF: communication networks, 4,519 answers, model answers, marks, and human-written feedback. Open licence.
  • Mohler: data structures, about 2,200 answers, model answers, marks 0 to 5 from two teachers.
Why these first
Free, small, and already carry the teacher's marks, so we can score ourselves with zero labelling. SAF also gives teacher feedback to compare against. The answers are short, so runs are cheap and fast.
What we build
The whole six-step pipeline, the automatic checks, and the evaluation script.
Done when
Our marks clearly beat the simple one-prompt baseline, and the feedback scores well on the automatic feedback checks. We set the exact numbers after the first baseline run.
STAGE 2

STEM with working

Maths, physics, chemistry
Datasets
  • Question banks for the questions and solutions: SciBench (college physics, chemistry, maths), UGMathBench (undergraduate maths). First check that SciBench includes full worked solutions; if not, the AI drafts them and we verify against the final answer.
  • Practice answer sheets we generate: a different AI plays a student and writes answers of varying quality with planned mistakes, such as a wrong formula, a unit slip or a missing step. Because we planned the mistake, we know the right mark without anyone labelling it.
  • ProcessBench and PRM800K: maths solutions with the first wrong step already marked by experts. We use these to test whether our marking finds the right mistake.
  • RiceChem: chemistry long answers with marking points. Needs an access request, so it is a bonus, not a dependency.
What is new
Marks for method, not just the final answer. Carrying a wrong number forward without being penalised twice. Equivalent answers that look different (units, forms of an equation).
Limits
There is no real graded physics data we could find, so physics results come from generated sheets only. Generated answers are tidier than real ones, so we treat those results as indicative.
Done when
Marks match planned marks on generated sheets, ProcessBench mistakes are located correctly most of the time, and feedback holds up on the same checks as stage 1.
STAGE 3

Harder formats

Handwriting, circuits, longer derivations
Datasets
EDU-CIRCUIT-HW: 1,334 handwritten circuit solutions with rubrics and teacher marks. Problem statements are withheld by the authors and need a request.
Approach
Add a first step where a vision-capable model turns the photo into text, then reuse the same pipeline. We measure that step separately so we know whether a lost mark came from misreading or from misjudging.
Start only when
Stages 1 and 2 are stable.
STAGE 4

Other fields

Optional
Status
Nothing outside STEM stood out in our search. A dataset qualifies if it has model answers or a marking scheme plus teacher marks. We will look again once stage 2 works.

6. How we know it works, without labelling anything

Marks

Compare our marks with the teacher's on the held-out questions. Report two things: how often we give the same mark, and how far off we are on average. Also report whether we lean strict or lenient. Where the dataset has two teachers, their agreement with each other is the best we can expect.

Feedback

A different AI scores each piece of feedback on five yes or no questions:

  1. Does it point at what the student actually wrote?
  2. Does it say which marking point was missed?
  3. Does it explain why it is wrong?
  4. Does it say what to do next?
  5. Is it correct?

For SAF we also compare with the teacher's own feedback.

Marking schemes

Three automatic tests per generated scheme: the model answer should get full marks, a blank or irrelevant answer should get none, and the marks should add up to the question total. Schemes that fail are regenerated once.

An extra test for "can a student improve from this?"

A model rewrites the student's answer using only our feedback, and we mark the rewrite. If the feedback was useful, the marks on the pointed-out parts should rise. This is our own idea, not a proven method, so we first check it against your own reading of a few examples before trusting it.

Your part: read about 20 outputs per milestone and say whether the feedback would help you. That is the final judge, and it takes minutes, not annotation days.

7. Risks

Middle-quality answers

Half-right answers are where humans and AI disagree most. We report results separately for weak, middle and strong answers so this does not hide.

Generated practice sheets

Cleaner than real students. Good for finding bugs and testing feedback, weaker as proof of accuracy. Real datasets give the final marks numbers.

Memorised questions

Public question banks may be in the model's training data, so it may know the answer. We change numbers in questions where we can.

Teachers disagree

If two teachers differ by a mark, no system can be exact. We compare against that gap, not against perfection.

Thin evidence

Research claims we rely on come from single papers, often small and on older models. Treat them as starting guesses and let our own results overrule them.
